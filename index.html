    A2.1 · A2.2.12–14

    Origins of cells

    How you get from a puddle of inorganic chemistry to something that can keep itself alive, copy itself, and eventually build a caribou.

    What counts as alive

    Cell theory says the cell is the smallest unit of life, all living things are made of cells or cell products, and cells only come from pre-existing cells. That last point is what creates the puzzle: the first cell had nothing to come from.

    A living thing is self-sustaining, meaning it carries out all the functions of life on its own: metabolism, reproduction, sensitivity, homeostasis, excretion, nutrition, growth, and movement. Nothing smaller than a cell manages the whole list.

    Viruses fail the test. They have no metabolism of their own, cannot replicate without hijacking a host, and do not grow. That makes them non-living, even though they carry genetic material.

    Conditions on early Earth (A2.1.1)

    • Reducing atmosphere with essentially no free oxygen, so no ozone layer and no oxidation of new organic molecules.
    • Intense UV radiation reaching the surface, supplying free energy for chemical reactions.
    • Volcanic activity releasing CH4, CO2, ammonia and water vapour into the atmosphere.
    • High temperatures from volcanism and a greenhouse effect, speeding up reactions.
    • Liquid water as a solvent and reagent.
    Exam trap

    Oxygen is the giveaway. An oxygen-rich early atmosphere would have oxidised organic molecules as fast as they formed, so "a reducing atmosphere" is the correct condition, never "abundant free oxygen".

    Abiogenesis, in four steps (A2.1.3)

    1. Non-living synthesis of simple organic monomers from inorganic molecules.
    2. Assembly of those monomers into polymers.
    3. Some polymers gain the ability to self-replicate.
    4. Replicating molecules become packaged inside membranes, forming protocells.

    This is hard to test directly. Pre-biotic conditions cannot be reproduced exactly, and protocells left no fossils, so the evidence is indirect and inferential.

    Miller–Urey (A2.1.4)

    Miller and Urey sealed a flask system that modelled early Earth: water boiled to vapour, a mixture of gases with no oxygen, an electrical discharge standing in for lightning, and a condenser to cool the mixture back to liquid. After a week the condensed liquid contained amino acids and other organic molecules.

    Exam trap

    The experiment showed that organic molecules can form from non-living materials. It did not produce cells, did not produce life, and said nothing about RNA replication. Any answer claiming cells formed is wrong.

    Protocells and membranes (A2.1.5)

    Fatty acids in water spontaneously form micelles, because the hydrophobic tails cluster together to avoid the polar solvent. When polar organic monomers are present they interact with the glycerol/phosphate heads, and the structure rearranges into a spherical bilayer, or vesicle.

    The point of a membrane is separation. Once a hydrophobic barrier exists, the chemistry inside the compartment can differ from the chemistry outside, which is the precondition for metabolism.

    RNA world (A2.1.6)

    A self-sustaining system needs a molecule that stores information and a molecule that catalyses reactions. In modern cells those jobs are split between DNA and proteins, which creates a chicken-and-egg problem: proteins are built using instructions in DNA, and DNA is replicated by protein enzymes. Neither could come first.

    RNA solves it because it does both. It carries a base sequence, it can self-replicate, and some RNAs are catalytic (ribozymes, and the catalytic core of the ribosome). RNA is therefore presumed to be the first genetic material, with DNA taking over later for stability and proteins taking over catalysis for diversity.

    Water: why it matters and where it came from (A1.1.7–8)

    • Polarity drives non-polar lipids to form bilayers spontaneously.
    • High specific heat capacity buffers temperature, supporting stable internal conditions.
    • It dissolves polar and charged solutes, so reagents can collide and react.
    • It is a reagent itself in condensation and hydrolysis reactions.

    Earth was probably too hot and too low in mass early on to hold onto water vapour, so much of it is thought to have arrived on asteroids and comets carrying ice, once Earth had cooled and gained enough mass to retain it. This is why the search for extraterrestrial life focuses on liquid water and on the Goldilocks zone: the orbital band around a star where a planet is neither too hot nor too cold for liquid water. Hotter stars push that band further out. The planet also needs enough mass and a suitable atmosphere to keep the water on the surface.

    LUCA (A2.1.7–9)

    LUCA is the last universal common ancestor: the inferred population of cells from which all life today descends. It is not the first cell (that would be FUCA), and other lineages almost certainly existed alongside it and went extinct.

    Evidence that LUCA existed

    1. Universality of the genetic code. Almost every organism reads the same codons as the same amino acids, with only minor variations.
    2. Conserved gene sequences. A core set of genes is shared across bacteria and archaea, which is hard to explain unless they were inherited from a common ancestor.

    What conserved genes suggest LUCA was like

    An anaerobe (oxygen was absent and would have been toxic), a chemoautotroph (energy from oxidising inorganic chemicals rather than from light or from eating others), and a thermophile (adapted to high temperature). Together these point to hydrothermal vents.

    Dating the first cells

    • Biosignatures: chemicals produced only by living processes, found in rock layers that can be radiometrically dated.
    • Stromatolites: layered sedimentary structures built by microbial mats. The oldest are roughly 3.45 billion years old.
    • Molecular clocks: counting differences in conserved sequences to estimate time since divergence, assuming a roughly constant mutation rate.
    • Fossilised bacteria in mineral precipitates around ancient vents date to about 3.7 billion years.
    Watch the wording

    Horizontal gene transfer means the LUCA "genome" is best read as representative of a whole community of early organisms, not one individual cell.

    Endosymbiosis (A2.2.12)

    Eukaryotes are thought to have arisen when one prokaryote engulfed another and did not digest it. The engulfed aerobic bacterium became the mitochondrion; a later engulfment of a photosynthetic bacterium produced the chloroplast. Membrane in-folding of the host produced the nucleus and endomembrane system.

    Evidence, remembered as MAD DR:

    AntibioticsSusceptible to antibiotics that target bacteria.
    DNATheir own DNA, which is naked and circular like bacterial DNA.
    DivisionReproduce by a fission-like process, independently of the cell.
    Ribosomes70S ribosomes, the prokaryotic size, not the eukaryotic 80S.

    Multicellularity and differentiation (A2.2.13–14)

    Multicellularity has evolved repeatedly, and it pays off in three ways: organisms can exceed the size limit imposed by surface area to volume ratio, they can outlive their individual cells, and they can specialise cell types. Groups of cells acting together produce emergent properties that no single cell has.

    Specialisation happens through differential gene expression. Every cell keeps the whole genome; what differs is which genes are switched on, often in response to chemical or environmental signals. The cost of multicellularity is cancer, which is what happens when one cell's growth stops being controlled.

    B2.1.11–17

    Cell membranes

    A fluid, selectively permeable bilayer that decides what gets in, what gets out, and how cells stick to each other.

    The bilayer, quickly

    Phospholipids are amphipathic: a polar, hydrophilic phosphate head and two non-polar, hydrophobic fatty acid tails. In water they assemble into a bilayer with the tails facing inward, held together by hydrophobic interactions. Proteins are embedded in and on the bilayer. The membrane is fluid, so components drift laterally, and selectively permeable, so small non-polar molecules cross freely while large or charged particles need help.

    Fatty acid composition and fluidity (B2.1.11)

    SaturatedUnsaturated
    StructureNo C=C double bonds, straight tailsOne or more C=C double bonds, kinked tails
    PackingPack tightly togetherKinks prevent tight packing
    Effect on membraneLess fluid, more rigidMore fluid
    Melting pointHigherLower

    Organisms adjust the ratio to match their environment. Antarctic cod living in freezing water have a high proportion of unsaturated fatty acids so their membranes stay fluid rather than freezing solid. Caribou have more unsaturated fatty acids in cells at their extremities, where it is coldest. Some bacteria produce desaturase enzymes to add double bonds when temperatures drop.

    Exam trap

    Cold-adapted membranes are not being "strengthened" or "stabilised" by unsaturation, and unsaturation lowers rather than raises the melting point. The whole point is maintaining fluidity at low temperature by preventing tight packing.

    Cholesterol (B2.1.12)

    Cholesterol sits between phospholipids in animal cell membranes and works in both directions:

    • At high temperatures it restrains phospholipid movement, so it reduces fluidity and keeps the membrane from becoming too permeable.
    • At low temperatures it wedges between tails and stops them packing into a solid gel, so it increases fluidity.

    That two-way action is why it is described as a bidirectional modulator, or a fluidity buffer.

    Vesicular transport (B2.1.13)

    Because the bilayer is fluid it can break and reseal, which is what makes vesicle traffic possible.

    ProcessDirectionWhat movesExample
    PhagocytosisInSolid materialA leukocyte engulfing a pathogen
    PinocytosisInDissolved material and fluidIntestinal cells taking in dissolved nutrients
    ExocytosisOutVesicle contentsNeurons releasing neurotransmitter into the synapse

    Endocytosis brings material in without it crossing the bilayer, which is the reason large particles can enter at all.

    Directional transport: uniport, symport, antiport (B2.1.14–16)

    TypeDefinitionWorked example
    UniportOne solute, one directionGLUT transporters moving glucose into red blood cells; voltage-gated K+ channels; ligand-gated Na+ channels
    SymportTwo solutes, same directionSodium-dependent glucose cotransporter (SGLT) in intestine and kidney; H+/K+ uptake in plant roots

    Gated ion channels in neurons

    Voltage-gated channels open in response to a change in membrane potential — potassium channels opening during repolarisation, for instance. Ligand-gated channels open when a signalling molecule binds; the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor on a muscle fibre is the standard example, and the ion influx through it triggers contraction.

    Sodium–potassium pump (direct active transport)

    1. Three Na+ from the cytoplasm bind to the pump.
    2. ATP hydrolysis phosphorylates the pump, changing its conformation and expelling the Na+.
    3. Two K+ from outside bind.
    4. The phosphate is released, the pump returns to its original shape, and K+ enters the cell.

    Net result per cycle: 3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in, one ATP spent. It is an antiporter and it is directly active because ATP is hydrolysed by the transporter itself.

    SGLT (indirect active transport)

    The Na+/K+ pump keeps intracellular Na+ low, creating an electrochemical gradient. The cotransporter lets Na+ fall down that gradient and uses the energy released to drag glucose against its concentration gradient into the cell. No ATP is hydrolysed by the cotransporter, so this is indirect active transport. It happens in the small intestine and kidney tubules.

    Exam trap

    "No ATP used at this protein" does not make it passive. Glucose is moving against its gradient, so it is still active transport, just powered second-hand by a gradient that ATP built.

    Cell adhesion and junctions (B2.1.17)

    Cell adhesion molecules bind cells to each other and to the extracellular matrix, which is the protein scaffold surrounding cells. Adhesion is what makes tissues, and therefore multicellularity, possible.

    JunctionFunction
    Anchoring junctionsHold cells together mechanically within a tissue layer
    Tight junctionsCreate an impermeable seal between adjacent cells, blocking leakage between them
    Gap junctionsChannels that let ions and small molecules pass directly between linked cells
    PlasmodesmataThe plant equivalent of gap junctions, through the cell wall

    Loss of adhesion is why malignant tumours metastasise: cells that no longer stick to their neighbours can detach, enter the blood or lymph, and seed secondary tumours elsewhere. Note that loss of growth control and loss of adhesion are two different failures — metastasis specifically requires the adhesion failure.

    B2.2.4–8 · B2.3.7–10

    Cell structures and specialisation

    Compartments with their own internal chemistry, and cells whose shape is an argument about their job.

    Why compartmentalise

    A membrane-bound organelle can hold a different pH, different enzymes and different concentrations from the cytoplasm. That lets incompatible reactions run at the same time in one cell, and lets each process happen under its optimum conditions.

    The nucleus and its double membrane (B2.2.6)

    • Two membranes create the space for nuclear pores, which allow selective two-way traffic: histones and transcription factors in, mRNA and tRNA out.
    • The envelope can disassemble into vesicles during prophase and reassemble into two nuclei at telophase, which a single membrane could not do as cleanly.
    • It is continuous with the ER, linking the nucleus to the endomembrane system.

    Free versus bound ribosomes (B2.2.7)

    Both types have a small subunit that binds mRNA and a large subunit that binds tRNA, and both assemble at the start of translation. The difference is destination.

    • Free ribosomes float in the cytosol and make proteins that stay and work in the cytosol.
    • rER-bound ribosomes make proteins that enter the endomembrane system: secreted proteins, membrane proteins, and enzymes destined for organelles such as lysosomes.
    Exam trap

    Free ribosomes are not fixed in place, and they are not the ones making secreted protein. Reverse those two claims and you have the two commonest distractors.

    Golgi apparatus and secretion (B2.2.8)

    The Golgi is a stack of cisternae between the ER (cis face) and the plasma membrane (trans face). Each sac carries out a specific modification as material moves through, and the Golgi sorts and exports secretory proteins, glycoproteins, membrane proteins, glycolipids and plant cell wall material.

    • Constitutive secretion: vesicles are released continuously, with no signal required.
    • Regulated secretion: vesicles are stored and released only when a chemical signal (ligand) arrives, allowing a controlled or sustained release.

    Vesicles and clathrin (B2.2.8)

    Vesicles store material and shuttle it between compartments, moved along the cytoskeleton by motor proteins such as kinesin and dynein. Lysosomes, peroxisomes and vacuoles are all vesicle types.

    1. Adaptor proteins mark the patch of membrane where a vesicle should form, in response to ligand signalling.
    2. Clathrin, a triskelion-shaped coat protein, is recruited and links into a lattice-like cage.
    3. The cage curves the membrane into a bud.
    4. Dynamin pinches the neck and cleaves the bud free.
    5. The clathrin coat dissociates and is reused; the vesicle travels on.
    Exam trap

    Clathrin shapes the bud and then leaves. It is not a channel, it does not stay permanently in the membrane, and it is not the motor that walks vesicles along microtubules.

    Surface area to volume ratio (B2.3.7)

    Exchange happens across a surface, but demand scales with volume. As a cell grows, volume rises faster than surface area, so the ratio falls and exchange becomes limiting. Cells specialised for exchange fight this two ways: reduce diffusion distance by being flat and thin, and increase surface area with projections. Examples include squamous cells, biconcave red blood cells, invaginated kidney tubules, and microvilli on tubule and intestinal cells.

    Pneumocytes (B2.3.8)

    • Type I: extremely thin squamous cells forming most of the alveolar surface, giving a short diffusion path for gas exchange.
    • Type II: cuboidal cells that secrete surfactant, which lowers surface tension and stops alveoli collapsing.
    Exam trap

    The pair is nearly always tested by swapping the two. Type I is thin and for gas exchange; Type II makes surfactant.

    C2.1.1–14

    Cell signalling

    One molecule arrives at a cell surface and a million molecules inside change behaviour. This is the topic with the most moving parts, so get the vocabulary exact.

    The core sequence

    Signal → reception → transduction → response. A ligand is the chemical messenger that starts it. A receptor is a protein with a binding site complementary to that ligand; binding causes a conformational change in the receptor, which triggers the pathway. Transduction is the conversion of the chemical signal into a cellular response, usually through a relay that amplifies it.

    Vocabulary check

    The ligand is the signal, not the receptor and not the G protein. In an adrenaline question, adrenaline is the ligand, the GPCR is the receptor, GTP is a nucleotide that switches the G protein on, and cAMP is the second messenger.

    Quorum sensing (C2.1.2)

    Bacteria coordinate behaviour by population density. Each cell releases a ligand called an autoinducer. As the population grows, autoinducer concentration rises, and once it passes a threshold it triggers transcriptional changes across the whole population at once.

    Vibrio fischeri uses quorum sensing to produce light. It lives inside the bobtail squid in a mutualism: the bacteria get organic nutrients, and the squid gets counterillumination camouflage that hides its silhouette from predators below.

    Range of signalling (C2.1.5)

    Chemically, hormones and neurotransmitters come in several classes: amines (adrenaline, thyroxin, melatonin, dopamine, serotonin), peptides/proteins (insulin, glucagon, ADH, GnRH, endorphins), lipids/steroids (oestradiol, progesterone, testosterone, cortisol), and others including gases (nitric oxide) and amino acids (glutamate). Every ligand must be soluble in the transport medium or carried by a hydrophilic carrier protein.

    Receptor structure follows from this. Intracellular receptors have hydrophilic outer surfaces (they sit in cytoplasm) with non-polar binding sites. Transmembrane receptors have non-polar surfaces facing the lipid bilayer and polar residues facing the extracellular fluid, where the binding site is.

    Intracellular receptors and steroids (C2.1.12–13)

    1. The steroid diffuses across the plasma membrane.
    2. It binds an intracellular receptor, forming an activated complex.
    3. The complex moves into the nucleus and binds a specific DNA sequence.
    4. Acting as a transcription factor, it changes gene expression, and the new proteins produce the cellular response.

    Oestradiol binds receptors in hypothalamus cells and regulates GnRH secretion, which controls FSH and LH release from the pituitary. Progesterone binds receptors in endometrial cells and promotes proliferation, thickening the uterine lining.

    Transmembrane 1: ligand-gated ion channels (C2.1.8)

    1. Acetylcholine is released from the neuron into the synaptic cleft.
    2. It binds nicotinic receptors on the muscle fibre membrane, opening ligand-gated Na+ channels.
    3. Na+ flows in and the membrane depolarises.
    4. Depolarisation triggers Ca2+ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, causing contraction.
    5. Acetylcholine is broken down and recycled, preventing overstimulation.

    Transmembrane 2: G-protein coupled receptors (C2.1.9–10)

    A GPCR spans the membrane, with an extracellular ligand-binding site and an intracellular region associated with a G protein made of α, β and γ subunits. At rest the α subunit holds GDP.

    1. The ligand binds the extracellular site and the receptor changes shape.
    2. The receptor catalyses exchange of GDP for GTP on the α subunit, which activates the G protein.
    3. The α–GTP subunit dissociates from the β–γ dimer.
    4. Both parts diffuse laterally through the membrane and regulate effector proteins.
    5. GTP is eventually hydrolysed back to GDP, the subunits reassociate, and the signal switches off.

    Adrenaline, worked through

    Adrenaline (the ligand) binds a GPCR. The activated G protein activates adenylate cyclase, a membrane-bound enzyme, which converts ATP into cyclic AMP. cAMP is the second messenger, and because one receptor activates many G proteins and one enzyme makes many cAMP molecules, the signal is amplified enormously. The effects add up to fight-or-flight: pupil dilation, increased ventilation, glycogen breakdown and greater muscle activity.

    Exam trap

    The α subunit does not detach from the membrane and does not enter the nucleus, and the whole G protein does not float off into the cytoplasm. It dissociates from β–γ and both pieces stay in the membrane.

    Transmembrane 3: receptor tyrosine kinases (C2.1.11)

    An RTK is a transmembrane enzyme. When the ligand binds, receptor monomers pair up and the intracellular tails phosphorylate each other: the kinase transfers phosphate groups from ATP onto tyrosine residues. The phosphorylated tails become docking sites that recruit cytosolic relay proteins, and a cascade follows.

    Insulin, worked through

    1. Insulin binds the extracellular site of the insulin receptor.
    2. The two intracellular tails are phosphorylated on tyrosine residues using phosphate from ATP.
    3. Relay proteins dock at the phosphorylated tails and trigger a reaction series.
    4. Vesicles containing GLUT glucose transporters move to and fuse with the plasma membrane.
    5. More transporters in the membrane means faster facilitated diffusion of glucose into the cell, lowering blood glucose.
    Exam trap

    Insulin never touches the glucose. It does not open a hole in the bilayer, does not convert glucose to glycogen directly, and the receptor does not transport glucose. It just increases how many transporters are in the membrane.

    What signals actually do to target cells (Target Effects)

    • Change gene expression patterns, which can drive differentiation.
    • Regulate enzyme activity, changing rates of metabolic reactions.
    • Change cell shape or size, including muscle contraction.
    • Change rates of cell division or of apoptosis.

    Signalling molecules are not converted into ATP or used as an energy source. That is a standard distractor.

    Feedback (C2.1.14)

    Negative feedback reduces the original stimulus and returns the system toward a set point; it is the basis of homeostasis. Positive feedback amplifies the stimulus and drives the system further from the starting point, which is useful when you want a process to run to completion, such as childbirth or an action potential.

    A2.3.1–6

    Viruses

    Not alive, extremely good at it anyway.

    What every virus has (A2.3.1)

    • A capsid, a protein coat enclosing the particle.
    • Genetic material, either DNA or RNA, never both.
    • No cytoplasm, and few or no enzymes of its own.
    • A small, fixed size with no capacity for growth, roughly 20–300 nm.

    Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites: they commandeer host ribosomes, enzymes and ATP to build new virions. Lacking metabolism, growth and independent reproduction is exactly why they are classed as non-living.

    How viruses differ (A2.3.2)

    • Capsid shape: helical, polyhedral/icosahedral, spherical or complex.
    • Attachment proteins: distinct glycoproteins that bind complementary host receptors, which determines host range and tissue specificity.
    • Genome: DNA or RNA, single- or double-stranded, linear or circular.
    • Envelope: some are wrapped in host-derived lipid membrane, some are naked.

    Replication cycles (A2.3.3–4)

    Lytic

    1. Attachment to complementary receptor proteins on the host.
    2. Entry and replication: viral genome enters and host machinery makes viral nucleic acid and proteins.
    3. Assembly of component parts into new virions.
    4. Release: enzymes degrade the membrane, the cell lyses and is destroyed, releasing virions.

    Lysogenic

    After attachment, an enzyme catalyses integration of the viral sequence into the host genome. The cell then divides normally, copying the viral sequence into every daughter cell — this is latency, and no virions are made. A stressor triggers induction, switching the virus to the lytic cycle and destroying the whole infected cell line.

    Exam trap

    Lysogeny does not kill the cell at once, and it spreads the viral genome through cell division rather than through virion release. Immediate lysis is the lytic cycle.

    Where viruses came from (A2.3.5)

    • Virus-first: viruses were self-replicating units before cells. Considered unlikely, since viruses need cells to replicate.
    • Escape (progressive): viruses began as transposable genetic elements inside cells that escaped and became encapsulated.
    • Regressive: viruses were once cells living symbiotically or parasitically that progressively lost components until only the genome and coat remained.

    The universality of the genetic code links viruses to cellular life. The sheer diversity of virus types suggests several separate origins, and the features they all share may be convergent evolution under the same selection pressure of obligate parasitism. Origins are hard to settle because viruses are too fragile to fossilise.

    Rapid evolution (A2.3.6)

    • High mutation rates: RNA and single-stranded DNA are less stable, and viruses lack proofreading enzymes. HIV averages about 0.34 mutations per replication cycle.
    • Short generation times: an infectious cycle takes roughly 8–72 hours; influenza is under 12.
    • Large populations: each cycle can release around 10,000 virions, so even rare mutations appear often.

    CauseAccumulated point mutationsReassortment of gene segments when one cell is infected by two viruses

    Consequences for control: vaccines must be reformulated as strains change, infected individuals may be quarantined, vectors can be targeted (chlorination, fumigation), and genetic engineering can produce disease-resistant or sterile vectors.
